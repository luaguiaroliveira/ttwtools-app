<EPENTHESIS’ USE AMONG BRAZILIANS LEARNERS OF ENGLISH>

In Portuguese consonants do not usually appear in word-final position and according to Cristófaro Silva observation, an extra vowel is susceptible to be inserted by Brazilian learners of English; this phenomenon is known as Epenthesis. Epenthesis occurs with all the speakers, mainly with a second language learner, and it can make a word unintelligible to natives. Learning the phonetic alphabet is a way to understand and avoid this phenomenon. This essay will focus on Brazilian English learners, providing datas and it will explain why Epenthesis happens.
According to Cristófaro Silva (2012), in phonology and phonetics, epenthesis is the insertion of an extra sound in the pronunciation of a word to facilitate the articulation of the sounds. It may be accidental, or a vice of speech. Brazilians are used to words ending in s, r and l. When a word ends in a different consonant from these ones, an extra vowel is likely to be inserted. For example, the pronunciation of the word “Stop” in English ends in consonant [p], but Brazilians are used to inserting the vowel [i] because do not exists words in Portuguese that ends in [p]. In addition, this occurs in several words. This [i] may be inserted in word-final position after the fricatives /f/, /v/, /S/ and /Z/.
The production of Epenthesis may happen at the beginning of words too, for the same reason, words in Portuguese tend to follow a rule: CVC (consonant, vowel, consonant) or VCV (vowel, consonant, and vowel); however, in English some words begin with consonant which are followed by another consonant. An example of this case is; Street, Brazilians may insert a vowel before the consonant S to follow the Portuguese rule, [istrit?i]. This phenomenon may occur in several words.
Some research have been done about the occurrence of Epenthesis, a lot of that to show if a word may be interpreted in a wrong way or be unintelligible to natives. Cruz (2003) has conducted a research that investigated how many errors at a segment level (consonants, vowels, epenthesis and word stress) influence Brazilian learners’ unintelligibility to native speakers. In relation to epenthesis, none of the seven identified occurrences were judged as impossible to understand, although the word ‘small’ (pronounced [iz`m?w]) and one other case were considered to be difficult to understand. She concluded that epenthesis alone is not sufficient to cause unintelligibility, and that it is possible to suggest that the voicing of the initial fricative in the cluster /sm/ is probably unintelligible for native speakers.
Silveira (2007) has conducted a similar research, in which Brazilian EFL adult learners had to do a free-speech task and a read aloud one. Her research aimed to study the role played by orthography on the difficulties some people face when pronouncing words in English that end in consonants. Silveira concluded, regarding epenthesis, that ,due to the structure of the Brazilian Portuguese syllable, speakers tend to insert a vowel so it will match their own language better, also, considering that most cognates in English that end with a consonant in Portuguese have a vowel after it, (e.g. nostalgic and nostálgico).
Cruz (2008) also conducted a research on whether epenthesis is a source of unintelligibility or not. The author divided the listeners into three groups and asked them to listen to the audios of Brazilians speaking English and then do tasks. One of these tasks was being to write what they had heard. 56% of the transcriptions was not, but the rest (44%) were wrongly written, which led Cruz to state that vowel insertion might make the speech unintelligible. She concluded that the most intelligible epenthesis occurrence is the reduced [i] vowel (e.g. live), followed by the full [i] vowel. (E.g. leave).
Epenthesis is common, but Brazilians can practice and lose this trend of Brazilian Portuguese, learning the phonetic transcription, for example. Phonetic transcription can avoid learners being misled by an incapably trained ear or by the effect of the ordinary spelling; therefore, learning phonetic transcription enables the language learner to extract precise pronunciation from a dictionary or online. Epenthesis occurs in all languages, but the focus here was with Brazilians, because of the trend to insert vowel following Portuguese rules.
