Biotechnology is a field that evolved in the second half of the last century and it is a hot topic in the news, in science, and even in art. The attention towards biotechnology is probably wrapped around the unprecedented revolution it can lead to all aspects of human life. Cloning, extended lifespan, stem cell research, and genetic edition, once considered only by science fiction, are real approaches now. However, even scientists are not sure about how the future of mankind will look like in face of biotechnological advances. Whether it will look bright or not is beyond human sight; however, we must set today the limits for what can be done based on the events that happen in the present.
Clear laws have to be defined in biotechnological research. The policies concerning research ethical points vary widely among countries: in Iran, human embryonic stem cell research is allowed with no restrictions, while in the US each state has its laws (SANIEI 2013; URL 1). Even though UN has established the Universal Declaration on Bioethics and Human Rights, the resolutions only contribute to blur the line between right and wrong. For example, in its third article, it states that the individual well-being is more important than the interests of science and society (URL 2). However, is the (future) well-being of a fetus less important than the (potential) cure of aging? Is a fetus more important than an adult?
In addition, as research shows promising results, companies are developing new tools and products. However, their main objective can be profit and a vague set of laws could make justifiable a variety of actions. The pharmaceutical and biotech companies already make an extraordinary amount of money: it is estimated that Johnson & Johnson and Pfizer earned almost 25 billion of dollars in 2017 (URL 3). The actions of these huge companies are not comprised only to research or a few drugs we use in everyday life, but they also extend their tentacles to the government. An example of this is the lobby promoted by pharmaceutical companies in the US Congress: they spent 2.5 billion of dollars in campaign donation to politicians in the last decade (URL 4). In this regard, a set of laws that cannot be changed (a better biotechnological global constitution) should be formulated.
Another aspect concerns the legislation about devices and techniques that are not on the market yet, but are very likely to be in the upcoming years. In this case, an unambiguous legislation based on events happening now (or that happened in the past) must be applied.  One example is CRISPR, that allows gene editing in a fast and precise way. Although it is now confined to basic research, Chinese scientists have succeeded to edit the gene that causes congenital anemia using nonviable human embryos (LIANG, 2015). It is not hard to conceive this technique being used to enhance physical strength, to change the eye color, or to create an army of super soldiers. 
In conclusion, a direct, global, and strict set of laws must be enacted. As it was discussed, the laws regarding biotechnology vary greatly from country to country and even from state to state. In addition, as big companies and new techniques come to the global scenario, the discussion about what is legal would be relativized to fit in the interests of the investors. These reasons are more than enough to alarm every citizen in the world, as his or her rights could be endangered in a near future.
