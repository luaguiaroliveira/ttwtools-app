This essay will present and discuss the idea proposed in Alan Turing’s most cited academical work, namely, Computing Machinery and Intelligence. This work is claimed as one of the keystones upon which Artificial Intelligence (AI) has been developing since the advent of digital computers in the first half of the 20th century.
The first half of the past century was marked by strong concept proofs and developments in the Computer Science field, such as Turing Machines - a theoretical computational model capable of proving whether or not a given problem is computable -, the first general-purpose digital computer, and so on. Among the greatest names is Alan Turing, regarded as the father of modern computing and acknowledged by his direct and indirect contributions to the most varied fields of action. The work here to be discussed establishes one of the first statements regarding Artificial Intelligence.
In the paper, Turing proposes a simple test as a way to ensure whether or not a given computer is endowed with intelligence. The test comprises two players, a man (A) and a woman (B), and one digital computer. The players and the computer are physically separated and the test is performed in a question-answering fashion where the computer performs questions about any topic it finds useful to unveil the true identity of the individuals, however, one of the players’ goal is to provide misleading responses whereas the other one must always give correct answers. By the end of the endeavor, a computer is said intelligent if it was capable of realizing the individuals’ correct identities.
However, Alan Turing itself presents, as a counterpart, the argument of consciousness. For some, intelligence may be interpreted as a creative and inventive process strictly bonded to thoughts and emotions in such a way that no machine would ever be capable of developing. This contrastive point of view may seem a piece of evidence for the non-validity of the aforementioned Turing test and might even suggest that the only way one could conclude that machines think is through being a machine and recognizing itself as a thinking being, what we nowadays call Singularity. Likewise, one valid conclusion that can branch from this argument is that the only way to tell whether a human being thinks or not is to be that human being itself.
The intelligence test can be framed into a wide variety of displays related to interactions between humans and machines. One possible display of the test is a well-known approach named Viva Voce that is often applied to real-world situations. Viva Voce is a very specific type of exam in which, instead of answering written questions, the individuals are given spoken questions and their performances are evaluated based on their abilities to demonstrate sufficient knowledge about the specific subject. To cast the original formulation of Turing test in this type of exam, suppose instead of three parts, the test has now only two, the computer and one of the persons, either the man or the woman. In a spoken fashion, the human participant would be responsible for conjecturing questions about a topic whereas the machine has the obligation to answer the questions. A machine passes the test if it is capable of satisfactorily answer the formulation as inventively as a human would do.
Alan Turing’s perspective in the proposed work was that, by the end of the century, there would have such advancements in computational storage and power that it would be natural to speak about machine intelligence without even be criticized or questioned. However, nowadays, almost 70 years since his work was released, what can be said about the state of Artificial Intelligence systems? What are the main challenges? Was any system capable of passing Turing test? 
Indeed, as Turing expected, the advancements in processing and storage were quite remarkable in the last decades, however, not enough. With the advent of social media and mobile devices, the quantity of data produced daily (approximately 2.5 x 1018 bytes) is way above the current processing and storage capacity. Data is the key ingredient in building powerful artificial intelligence systems, nonetheless, contemporary approaches are still bounded by the available processors’ capacities and the ability to handle and gather representative data points to the construction of such systems. 
Actual AI systems are capable of satisfactorily perform a series of tasks like text/image classification, anomaly detection, clusterization, and so on, however, they have shown a poor performance regarding natural tasks for humans such as walking, identifying sarcasm, talking and speech recognition - the latter two specifically related to Turing’s test. Many companies like Google, Apple, IBM, Microsoft and Amazon have been trying to develop efficient ways to synthesize and understand speech at an acceptable level as a way to aggregate value to their final products and, one recent announcement from Google suggested that their artificial intelligence system passed the Turing test, a still difficult task, nonetheless, easier to be accomplished by Google due to its extensive amount of relevant. The main challenges yet to be unsolved are to develop a consistent way to build artificial intelligence systems without the necessity for big loads of data and to reduce cultural and negative biases (racism, misogyny, xenophobia, etc.) from the learning procedure.
