<Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) of LGBT people>

Generally, LGBT people face lower self-reported rates of health than their “cis-heterosexual” counterparts. They also present higher rates of heavy drinking, smoking, risky sex behavior, use and abuse of illicit drugs, anxiety, depression and suicide attempts. Even though it might be understood as a cause-consequence relationship, those indicators can also be seen more as consequences of the same root than any other association. There are innumerable reasons to believe that stigmatized social situation place them in a cycle of stress, violence and unhealthy coping strategies that lead them to poor health which accrues over time lowering progressively their health status and, consequently, their self perception of health. 
The understanding that social stigma and exclusion in LGBT people's lives reduces their quality of life is widely accepted in the literature (SANDFORT, GRAAF and BJIL, 2003). Although the majority of non-heterosexual / transgender men and women are healthy, this population may be at a greater risk of psychopathology, psychic suffering, and use/abuse of alcohol and other drugs. This is explained by the fact that members of minority groups face unique and chronic stressors because of their disadvantage in society, in this case materialized by discrimination, social stigma and internalized homonegativity. (HEREK, GARNETS, 2007).
Some recent studies have empirically demonstrated these relationships by: 1) identification of a higher incidence of depression, anxiety, alcohol/drugs use and abuse in the LGBT population (SANDFORT, GRAAF and BJIL, 2003; MUSTANSKI, GAROFALO and EMERSON, 2010; LEVASSEUR , KELVIN and GROSSKOPF, 2013, SANDFORT, GRAAF, HAVE, RANSOME and SCHNBEL, 2014, COCHRAN, BJOKENSTAM and MAYS, 2016, HOTTES, BOGAERT, RHODES, BRENNAN and GESINK, 2016, PEREIRA and COSTA, 2016, SEMLYEN, KING, VARNEY and HAGGER-JOHNSON, 2016); 2) finding a higher risk of maltreatment, physical and symbolic violence in this population (ROBERTS, AUSTIN, CORLISS, VANDERMORIS and KOENEN, 2010); 3) verification of higher rates of suicide attempts (MUSTANSKI, GAROFALO and EMERSON, 2010; COCHRAN, BJOKENSTAM and MAYS, 2016; KLEIN and GOLUB, 2016); and 4) detection of the difficulty of this population's access to health care due to the imposition of heteronormative attitudes by health professionals, which reduces the feeling of welcome and integral care (ALBUQUERQUE et al., 2016).
Based on those arguments we can attest that the health problems and the low quality of life that LGBT people faces is not a result of genetic or physiologic disadvantage which these populations has in their bodies. Those health markers are a result of a series of constrains and prejudice that puts LGBTs in a position of greater risk of violence and stigma. Therefore, the better to way to increase their health-related quality of life is to promote actions towards the reduction of the stigma and social acceptation.
