<Membrane separation process: the promising technology>

The technologies involving membrane separation process have been widely studied since the 1970’s, in order to replace conventional process distillation, filtration, absorption, and centrifugation, for instance. This process can offer several advantages in terms of energy consumption, specificity regarding of a particular product, and more flexibility in process scale-up. Some practical examples using membranes are water desalination and hemodialysis process. In both cases, the salt and minerals in water and blood, respectively, are removed with a membrane. This essay will explore how the membrane separation process works, focusing on ultrafiltration and microfiltration, and the application of this technology in the effluent treatment process and food industries.
The membrane is a solid film which operates as a selectivity barrier allowing the passage of certain components, according to their size, while the others are blocked (MIERZWA, 2005). There is a variety of membrane classified from their pore size range, as is the case for the microfiltration membranes which can filter solutes with sizes between 0.1 to 10 μm and ultrafiltration membranes among 10 to 1000 Å (MULDER, 1996). By contrast, the conventional filtration can only remove macroparticles with a size between 10 to 100 μm, not including viruses, bacteria, metal ions, and salts, for example (BAKER, 2004). Because of those characteristics, separation with membrane is utilized in several industrial processes such as food industry, effluent treatment, chemical, and biochemical processes, textile, etc (MOREIRA et al., 2011). 
The theme “water scarcity” is often discussed in the world seeking to find an efficient and fast solution for this issue. The treatment of effluents is a great way to minimize the water wastage. Sewage and industrial effluents are examples of waste which can be used when properly treated (GIACOBBO, et al., 2011). Since the membrane is capable to separate substances that conventional filter cannot as well as substitute decantation process, the result of microfiltration/ultrafiltration of this waste is a non-potable water (SCHAEFER, 2001). This water can normally use in sanitary discharges, irrigation, gardening, and street cleaning, for example. Therefore, making industries and government aware of this efficient process may be a major step towards reducing the use of clean water.
Food industries are always worried about producing foods with high nutritional and sensory quality. With this in mind, dairy industries are using ultrafiltration to increase the percentage of proteins in milk (RENNER, E. and ABD EL SALAM, M.H., 1991). This process permits to highlight the flavor and tasting of milk protein naturally (MODLER, 2000). Another process, which is frequently used in juice industries, is the pasteurization (inhibition of enzymatic reactions). Nevertheless, this process causes the loss of 12% of vitamin C in the acerola juice (MERCALI et al., 2012). Having say that, the ultrafiltration becomes a good alternative to produce clarified juice, because it preserves the nutritional proprieties and flavor, besides decreasing the degradative reactions of the fruit enzymes (PETRUS, J.C.C., 1997). So, the introduction of this technologies represents a gain in the quality of the final product.
In conclusion, membrane separation process is a recent technology present in several productive sectors, inasmuch as it can produce a final product, with more efficiency and quality. Being a selective solid film, the membrane allows to produce a non-potable water in a simple way from a wastewater, also milk and juices more naturally preserving their nutritional compounds. Technologies relate to membranes are being discussed and applied looking for a scenario of constant evolution.
