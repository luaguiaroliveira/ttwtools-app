Amerind languages are disappearing faster than we expected and this is worrying. Until 2030, around 44 to 60 of those languages will be extinct forever if nothing is done right now. So we must work in order to preserve those languages or at least information about those that will certainly be extinct, so we can use them as data to research about the genetic relationship among different languages. This essay aims to demonstrate why studying modern indigenous languages on a diachronic perspective can lead to amazing discoveries on how their protolanguage was like, its original homeland and later migrations.

First, giving complete description of modern languages and their classification in the same family is fundamental to the preservation of cultures that use them daily. Since many languages are disappearing at high speed, works in order to preserve them are important to be made, such as describing their grammar and giving their complete classification in a given family. Preserving a language also helps to maintain alive the customs of the people that speak it, since it is their vision of the world shared with younger generations. Therefore, a language is the tool of expression of a people.

Another reason is that reconstructing protolanguages should be done immediately. A protolanguage is a language spoken by a people before their divisions that led to different dialects and then into distinct languages. Also, it is reconstructed based on the comparison of its modern descendants. Without one of these descendants it is even more difficult to reconstruct a language we have no record of. It is quite clear that if a language disappears, we lose an important piece of the amazing puzzle that is a protolanguage.

Finally, after the reconstruction of a protolanguage, it will be possible to trace migrations that led to linguistic difference and then find out its original homeland. Analyzing differences among genetically related languages, we realize they tend to be regular and that changes even help to date the period in time when groups that spoke similar dialects split. Whenever a protolanguage is fully reconstructed, amazing discoveries are made, such as the customs of that people. For instance, if they had words for certain weapons, they were already capable of producing them. Also, their words for rites and traditions help us to know more about their ways of living.

In conclusion, studying modern indigenous languages on a diachronic perspective can lead not only to amazing discoveries on how their protolanguage was like, its original homeland and later migrations, but also discoveries on the pre-history of the Americas. Since many native peoples did not have writing or similar at the time Europeans arrived, it is difficult to have different perspectives on then other than those of invaders. Future works on historical linguistics will help us to know more about what those peoples were like and how amazing were their ways of life.
