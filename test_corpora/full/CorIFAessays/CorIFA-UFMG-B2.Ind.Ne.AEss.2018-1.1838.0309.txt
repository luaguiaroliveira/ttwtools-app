<Why do early interventions among children with Autism Spectrum Disorder?>

There is so much about autism in the traditional and social media nowadays. Almost one in a hundred children born are autistic with five times as many boys as girls (Frith, 2008). Although the number of diagnosis is currently high, we don’t really know the mechanisms behind the Autism Spectrum Disorder and even less how to deal properly with the wide range of the spectrum characteristics and difficulties. Despite the difficulties, early intervention models appear to provide a reliable path to improve the prognosis and everyday life and also to provide less cumulative losses during child development.        

The Autism Spectrum Disorder is described as a neurodevelopment disorder that is marked by a persistent pattern of deficits in social communication and social interaction across multiple contexts and also by restricted, repetitive patterns of behavior, interests or activities (DSM-V, 2013). The symptoms appear early in the developmental period and generally became clearer when the child reaches the age of two years. Multiple genetics studies found that the condition originates before birth and because of that it affects the whole development of the brain (Frith, 2008). However, their effect on the developing mind can be very different, originating a vastly different range of behaviors (Frith, 2008).

Because of the crucial development processes that occurs during the early ages it is crucial that it follows as closer of the normal path as possible, but autism spectrum disorder turns this development more unstable and difficult, so it is crucial to intervene effectively. By the moment, one of the major programs of intervention is the The Early Start Denver Model (ESDM) designed to target the key features of autism of children under the age of 5 years (Rogers & Dawson, 2010). The model sensitivity to the normal developmental sequence can have a more significative impact on the prognosis. 

In conclusion, due to the crucial developmental processes that occurs during the early ages and because the Autism Spectrum Disorder condition originates before birth and affects the whole development of the brain, early intervention models appears to provide a reliable path to improve the prognosis and everyday life by diminishing the cumulative losses during child development. Early interventions can also improve the prognosis of the condition by providing a greater maintenance of the gains obtained during the intervention, especially when the intervention starts even before the pattern of symptoms became clearer around the child’s age of two years.
