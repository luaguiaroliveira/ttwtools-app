<Regime Types. Which one is the best??>

The number of political regimes with democratic foundations has grown at the end of the twentieth century, raising questions about the characteristics of each type of regime and going deeper, seeking to identify the ideal type. But before thinking about which type would be best, it is necessary to clarify the characteristics of each type of regime. Although the dichotomy between presidentialism and parliamentarism has historically been the focus of academic work on constitution design, the choice of some countries, especially during the democratization process by hybrid systems, has drawn attention by highlighting its relevance.
During the process of (re)democratization , many issues related to institutional incentives for maintaining stability emerged because of the socioeconomic impact that systems changes may entail.  According to John Carey (2005), the major consequence of this transition is the attention focus on issues about constitutional rules, in other words, about the conformation of these new democracies. When the debate about the regime types started, Presidentialism was strongly criticized. The most influential author that promotes these critics was Juan Linz in his work “The Perils of Presidentialism”, in 1990. The author indicates Parliamentarism to the new Latin American democracies, because he believed that Presidentialism was more prone to democratic breakdown than Parliamentarism, as John Carey states: 
The central elements of Linz's case against Presidentialism are twofold. First, Presidentialism lacks Parliamentarism's safety valve, the confidence vote, that allows for the removal of a government from office in the event of a crisis without discarding the constitution. Second, Presidentialism creates incentives and conditions that encourage such crises in the first place, and particularly that aggravate the relationship between the executive and the legislature (Carey, 2005, p. 94).
Then, an academic consensus in favor of to Parliamentarism was generated by the time, nevertheless, the political world was moving to another direction  (Carey, 2005, p. 95). Briefly, the new democracies were established mostly in presidential or hybrid systems.
Many constitutional regimes combined elements from both regimes types – parliamentary and presidential, that is called hybrid, although the concept of this blend has been particularly prone to definitional problems (Elgie, 2004). As a way to unite the knowledge hitherto developed, Robert Elgie identified three types of definition of hybrid, which he called semi-presidentialism, in the literature. 
The three concepts exposed by Elgie show a progressive development of the theme. The second was based on Maurice Duverger’s view, which is divided into three parts: the election by universal suffrage of a president; this elected president has many powers; and, he has a prime minister and ministers as opposition. These first two conceptualizations have the figure of the president with power as a necessary criterion, which significantly reduces the number of countries that can be viewed as hybrids and <deleted citation> (Elgie, 2004, p.4). Therefore, the third type presented by Elgie (2004), which is the definition made by himself, should be defined by the dispositional properties of regime types alone, wherein the president must be popularly elected, and exist alongside a prime minister and cabinet. Lastly, this type of definition removes the characteristic of subjectivity of the analysis, because if requires no judgment about a powerful president or the opposition of the two branches.  
Even though there is no single concept about the hybrids, this system has grown around the world, as well as presidentialism itself, which contradicts the negative view of presidentialism, and parliamentary supremacy. As a result, the expansion of countries that present the plan called hybrid confirms Antonio Cintra’s view that the use of only two systems would be only a simplification (2007, 37). Understanding the constitutional choices that characterize regimes can ensure their maintenance and prevent democratic breakdowns.
As noted, many details influence the categorization of a regime type. Therefore, choosing one type as ideal over others can lead to institutional choices that generate instability. It is best to deeply understand the institutional and cultural reality of a system in order to (re)design the constitutional features of a regime.

The transition towards democracy, which has been called the “Third Wave of Democracy”, was composed of two main trends. The first one is the establishment of democracy in countries that did not have these experiences previously– this process is called democratization. The second one is the re-establishment of democracy in countries that are no longer a part of European and Soviet communism, and others that had periods of authoritarianism, such as Latin American countries – this process is called re-democratization.
