As our country still a democracy, Brazil’s population should be able to position about important decisions such as therapeutic potential of stem cell therapy, use of transgenic food and animal models at laboratories. According to OCDE [1], just 14% of Brazilians were graduated in 2014 and the power of taking supported decisions with reliable sources and information can be compromised.  At this scenario, scientists should be able to tackle the challenge of communication of science to the general public and provide skills to better understanding of our environment and society.
Although is expected from scientists the ability of communication through books and articles and all the expertise in analytical skills as well as research methodologies, it may be difficult communicating science or a specific topic to a layperson. One possible reason for this impasse is that people often can be not familiar with specialized terms and data analysis. For instance, in Brazil, when students left school, only 7,3% of them are proficient at mathematics. The scenario is worst at public schools, just 3,6% have satisfactory learning aims achieved at math. In other words, 96,4% of Brazilian students of public schools do not learn what is expected from them on this subject [2]. Another reason which can contribute to is the lack of formal training to scientists for the scientific spread of knowledge. To give an illustration, a few universities in Brazil have a module called science communication. Notably, Universidade Federal de São Paulo (USP) and Universidade Federal de Minas Gerais (UFMG) are providing specific disciplines dedicated to the theme [3]. However, the modules are not required for graduation and sometimes they may be only offered to masters and PhD students. As a consequence, it seems the scientists do not have the skill of communication and the majority of people are not prepared to hear.
Despite these issues concerned about the transmission of information, only relevant evidence is able to make people take informed decisions. To put it another way, unwise decisions are taken with poor knowledge about a topic. As an example, in our daily life, numerous situations require high quality information: a decision of buying an electronic device, which politician to be chosen, an intervention at the water treatment system, a new vaccine suggested by the government and even our way of feeding and workout. In addition, laypersons are the majority of society and they are able to take huge decisions as a community or a group. A plebiscite is perhaps the clearest example of the necessity of quality information to a society. In an opportunity to take an important political decision several opinions can be misguided by “fake news”, rumors and weak evidence. Therefore, besides the enormous challenge of bringing information to society, scientists must be able of positioning as the ones in charge of the task. 
In conclusion, the scientists must be able to spread the knowledge to society, not only by done solutions, tools or consumer goods but mainly providing information to supported decisions. It is expected from all scientists the ability of innovation, creativity and capacity of find new solutions in the way we communicate science to society.
