<What factors influence breathing pattern and thoracoabdominal motion during quiet breathing in healthy subjects?>

The normal respiratory movement that furthers the respiratory cycle consists of expansion and retraction of rib cage and abdomen, in a synchronic way, during inspiration and expiration, respectively (1). The breathing pattern is composed of variables related to respiratory times and volumes, and control of both seems to require an integrated and coordinated action of central, peripheral and muscular mechanisms (1,2). At rest, during quite breathing, is noticed that rib cage and abdomen move with considerable independence, accommodating different volumes harmonically.
It has been increasingly recognized that a detailed analysis of the breathing pattern and thoracoabdmominal motion can provide valuable information about the capacity of the respiratory system. As advances had been made in an attempt to elucidate the mechanisms involved in the control of breathing pattern and thoracoabdominal motion, improvements in instrumentation used for its evaluation were also developed (3,4,5).
Both breathing pattern and thoracoabdominal motion depend on the compliance of the compartments (6), and thus, are influenced by different factors such as position of the individual (7,8,9,10,12,13), sex (9,10,11,12), age (9,14,15), among others (16,17).
The influence of position on thoracoabdmominal motion of healthy subjects was extensively studied in the literature(7,8,9,10,12,13), being currently well established that posture promotes changes in the kinematics of chest wall during quiet breathing. Previous studies (7,8,9,10,12,13) have investigated the erected position (standing and sitting), supine, prone, lateral decubitus or varying degrees of trunk inclination in healthy subjects, through different instruments: mercury in silastic strain gauges (7); magnetometers (10,11); respiratory inductive plethysmography (9) and optoelectronic plethysmography (12,13). Despite the use of different measurement techniques, these authors (7,8,9,10,12,13) agreed to show that resting breathing is predominantly thoracic in erect positions and abdominal in supine position.
On the other hand, the effect of the change of posture on breathing pattern was little studied (8,12,13) and measurements were obtained by two different instruments: pneumotachograph8 and optoelectronic plethysmography (12,13). While Baydur et al. (8) did not observed changes of tidal volume and minute ventilation in the change of sitting posture to supine, Romei et al. (12) registered higher values of tidal volume and minute ventilation in seating position. Additionally, Priori et al.(13) did not observe changes in any variables of breathing pattern evaluated, with the change of the sitting position to supine. Of the four studies using optoelectronic plethysmography, two (18,19) analyzed the impact of the change in position just in thoracoabminal motion. Although Romei et al. (12) and Priori et al. (13) have assessed both breathing pattern as the kinematics of chest wall, were not described important variables of the breathing pattern such as: end-inspiratory chest wall volume, end-expiratory chest wall volume, ratio of inspiratory time to total time of the respiratory cycle and mean inspiratory flow in the study of Romei et al. (12). In addition, with regard to the thoracoabdominal motion, the response of the abdominal rib cage to different postures it was not evaluated and this is considered an important distinguishing factor of this instrument, in order that the forces acting on the upper rib cage (adjacent to lungs) are quite different from those acting on its bottom (adjacent to diaphragm). Finally, in the study of Priori et al. (13) only 12 subjects were analyzed and the study did not have as a primary objective the evaluation of the breathing pattern and thoracoabdominal motion response to the change of position.
The effects of gender on breathing pattern were little studied (12,20) and the influence on the kinematics of the chest wall remains controversial. The only studies (12,20) that assessed the influence of gender on breathing pattern, observed lower tidal volumes and minute ventilation in women in comparison with men. Additionally, regarding the thoracoabdominal motion, while some authors reported greater relative contribution of rib cage in women (10,12,21), others observed similar contributions in both sexes (9,11,20). Only the study of Romei et al. (12) used the optoelectronic plethysmography, however, the influence of sex on breathing pattern was restricted to the analysis of chest wall tidal volume, minute ventilation and respiratory rate. Furthermore, the evaluation of the effect of sex in abdominal rib cage has not been reported, and, according with our knowledge, has not been assessed in the literature. The possibility of the interaction between posture and sex on breathing pattern and thoracoabdominal motion during quite breathing was only proposed in this study (12), however, the analysis of this interaction has not been reported clearly.
Finally, regarding to age, all authors (9,14,15) in the literature reviewed, found no difference for the evaluated variables, suggesting that the aging process does not influence the breathing pattern and the thoracoabdominal motion of healthy individuals. Although, all studies have been used the respiratory inductive plethysmography as an evaluation instrument, and considering the limitations of this instrument, the measurement of the volume variations through the cross-sectional area appears to be prone to errors (20).
In conclusion, it is well known that posture influences the thoracoabdominal motion. The influence on breathing pattern, however, has been little studied. The effect of gender on breathing pattern was little explored and the influence on kinematics of the chest wall still controversial. Regarding to age, the literature suggests that aging does not change the breathing pattern and thoracoabdominal motion.
