<Coproduction of Public Services>

For the past years, it was common to assume that government had a one-dimensional role that was to collect taxes and deliver services. However, when government faces a crisis and there are not enough budgets to cover all the demands and needs of the citizens, some choices have to be done. For many years, public leaders believed that there were just two options to face this problem. The first is to raise taxes, and the second is to simply say no. To balance budgets and the provision of services, state and local government began to look for answers that lay somewhere between the traditional yes and no (Osborne and Gaebler, 1992). Some of those alternatives involve collaborative action between government and society in the provision of services. In this sense, coproduction is an important alternative for the provision of public services.

First, coproduction is important because it improves the efficiency of service process. In essence, coproduction involves an active role of clients, which are responsible for some activities in the service process, acting as partial employees. It means that some activities originally developed for the organization becomes client’s responsibility (Alford, 2009). As some examples can be mentioned the provision of information, fill of documents, use of client expertise to some decision making in the process. As pointed out for Parks et al. (1981), the most efficient mix of internal and external production depends on their relative costs. From this comes that by adopting coproduction, governments have opportunities to improve services’ efficiency and use the available resources in a more appropriate way.

Second, coproduction also contributes to the quality of services. When participating in an active way, clients are able to contribute for the services’ characteristics. The effective participation of the client can increase the probability that their needs are met, as well as it can generate a co-responsibility for the results of the service process (JO BITNER et al., 1997). By understanding the user as a partial employee, organizations expect to achieve higher quality and value added, and also greater propensity of clients to provide constructive feedback to improve services (Honebein; Cammarano, 2005).

Finally, coproduction is also important because it improves customers` satisfaction with the services provided. Honebein and Cammarano (2005) point out that by participating actively in the process, clients achieve greater satisfaction with the services and became more loyal to the organization and its services. Besides, some customers simply enjoy participating in service delivery (JO BITNER et al., 1997).

In conclusion, coproduction is an important alternative for the provision of public services, because it generates improvement of efficiency, quality and satisfaction. Considering that state and local government have to deal with restricted budgets that do not cover all the programs to attend the needs of society, working in a more collaborative way in the provision of public services by using coproduction brings out great opportunities.
