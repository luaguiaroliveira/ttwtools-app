<The importance of Mr. Keynes’ ideas for the economic thought>

The 2008 financial crisis evoked the return of a series of doubts in concern the limits of mainstream economic theory. The idea that economy follows a steady state in which all people willing to work have jobs — i.e. there is no involuntary unemployment — and that any deviation of this trajectory is naturally adjusted by the automatic forces of market was seriously contested, as well as the claim of dysregulated markets was discredited. Although died 62 years before the breakdown of the financial markets, Mr. Keynes has showed that the belief in automatic adjustment was nothing more than a faith, and that in a context of crisis the intervention of Government was necessary — just like it was in 2008. Compared only with the marginalist revolution — which laid the grounds of economic theory — and whilst controversial in academy, the keynesian revolution was a mark in the XX century, because it has showed the blindspots of neoclassical economics: these autors don’t considerate uncertainty, the liquidity preference or the possibility of equilibrium with subemployment.

In first place, one great contribution of Keynes for economic theory was the concept of uncertainty. Since there is a time gap between the decision of production by the firm and the collocation of the product in the market, the process of production is expectational. Therefore, once that in the decision moment the current information may not be sufficient to the most rational choice, uncertainty envolves production decisions of the agents. In face of long term choices, like a plan of investments for 10 years, firms can only conjecture about the comportament of market in the expected date (CARVALHO, 1992a: 177). Neoclassical theorists assume that the risk involved in this prospective plans can be calculated, i.e., the risk is probabilistic, while for Keynes it is not possible to get an distribution of probabilities and the subjective probability of the agent does not equals to the objective probability of the event (ANDRADE, 2011: 177). Economic agents just do not have the sufficient information to make a mathematical calculus.

Secondly, due to uncertainty, people consider as a rational choice keep their wealth in money or liquidity assets, like bank deposits. If the future is uncertain and agents do not share the belief that economy will grow, they will not risk their income, and will prefer stay “liquid”: while keeping illiquid assets, like machines or land, can make the proprietary lose with the depreciation, money has the propriety to maintain their value (in scenario of low inflation) and give flexibility to its possessor,  which can enjoy good opportunities (like buy an asset that will appreciate, or engage in the production of a commodity that is believed will be highly demanded). Then, if expectations are pessimistic, the flexibility and safety of money (called liquidity premium) will be higher than the yields offered by assets like investment goods, and the choice of liquid portfolios to allocate wealth will implicate low investment, low production, low demand of jobs and the economy, consequently, will contract (CARVALHO, 1992b: 93). Therefore, although orthodox economists declare that saving is good to economy, Keynes alleges the inverse.  

Finally, Keynes has shown that insufficiency of demand can keep economy below of its potential product and, hence, people willing to work will not find jobs. Once there is a portion of income that people don’t spend and is kept as liquid assets, the aggregate demand function has an inclination lower than aggregate supply function (DAVIDSON, 1999:58; CHICK, 1983:79), and is invalided the well-know Say Law — the process of production, due the various incomes spend (wages, interest rate), creates its own demand. Since demand does not equals to supply, it is possible that some production will not be performed, and then involuntary unemployment will arise; notwithstanding neoclassics supose all equilibrium was with full employment (the unemployment existing was only voluntary or temporary), Keynes pointed that was theoretically feasible an equilibrium with employment bellow the maximus.

The contributions of Mr. Keynes to economy, therefore, is multidimensional, and still today become present. The english economist showed to world that various premisses of orthodox economics were fragile, and attempted to tract theoretically  the real grounds of economic dynamics: the future is uncertain, to treasure (keep wealth in money) is a rational decision in this context and therefore the level of output can be below the most efficiency, opening space to government intervention.
