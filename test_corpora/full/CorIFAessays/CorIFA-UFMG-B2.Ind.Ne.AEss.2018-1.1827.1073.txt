<Healthcare Among Lesbian and Bisexual Women>

For years lesbian and bisexual women have been struggling with social invisibility. Since the 1970s they started fighting for civil rights along with the gay movement, but the L in the LGBT (Lesbian, Gay, Bisexual and Transgender) movement was only added during the 1980s. As a response to social movement requests, government policies started recognizing those identities existence and stablished guidelines to rule healthcare providers conducts. Still, many of them are dying from avoidable diseases. There’s much more to do in order to provide proper healthcare to this population.
Despite the political advances concerning the health of lesbian and bisexual women, healthcare professionals aren’t properly prepared to attend them. Curmi, Peters and Salamonson (2014) discuss the idea that sex between women doesn’t transmit diseases such as Human Papillomavirus (HPV). The authors reveal that the research participants heard from doctors that, as lesbians, they did not need the cervical cancer screening and prevention. According to the National Cancer Institute (INCA) 70% of the cervical cancers are related to HPV infections, data that confirms the high-risk lesbian and bisexual women are put in due this lack of knowledge about their health.
Agénor et al. (2015) point out that many women avoid talking about their sexual orientation, fearing negative reactions. They also discuss the poor professional involvement related to LGBT questions, supposing the patient’s heterosexuality. Consequently, this heterosexual centered treatment, with reproductive focus, neglects specificities of the lesbian/bisexual experience, offering insufficient healthcare. Surrounded by this non-receptive environment and feeling protected by the wrong idea that they aren’t at risk, those women tend to avoid medical appointments, increasing the probability of late cancer diagnosis, decreasing their chances of cure.
The data presented in this essay highlights the poor healthcare offered to lesbian and bisexual women. It is clear that the professionals are not prepared to attend them, proposing misguided treatments or reproducing homophobic conducts. To take better care of this population, offering proper treatment and chances of early cervical cancer diagnosis, it is necessary to improve healthcare providers formation and prepare them for the multiplicity they’ll find throughout their professional life.
