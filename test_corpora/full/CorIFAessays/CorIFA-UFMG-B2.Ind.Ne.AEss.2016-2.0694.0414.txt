<Some remarks on the meaning of the world>

<“_ But excuse-me, what do you think about this world? What thing is the world? ”>
<Canary's Ideas, Machado de Assis>

	World is a very common word. Everybody says all the time the word world, in various contexts and with several different meanings. World is a familiar word and, supposedly, a concept that we have acquaintance with. It is employed in the field of geopolitics, sports, cultures and others, either in common and academic language. But when we think radically about an all-encompassing concept like that, we can realize that we do not have a precise notion or definition to explain this experience of being in a world, or how it can be strange or even weird. Notwithstanding, we continue to refer to it as we say the “muslim world”, “latin world”, “third world”, the “ancient world”, the “globalized world”, “fictional worlds”, “virtual world”, etc, as the meaning of our expressions were clear. But in a close inspection, we are to face value at the mismatch between our confidence in our beliefs and the vagueness of our definitions.
Beyond the fact that all meanings can be integrated in our sense of world, we proceed like the bird of the story of the Brazilian writer Machado de Assis (1839-1908), who portraits a canary which is capable of thinking and speaking. Founded in a thrift store and questioned by an ornithologist amateur named Macedo about “what thing is the world?”, the canary presumes that himself is the owner of the world, which he defines based on the limits of his experiences: first he says that the world is a square cage on a thrift store; them, the world comes to be another cage, this time a circular and broader one, located on the balcony of a house, with its garden, its trees and its view to part of the blue sky, and, as the canary escaped to Macedo's house, to be refound after by a desperate man who lost his fantastic friend, the bird does not recognize anymore his previous definition of the world, which now seems to him a totally new thing: the world is now “the infinite and blue space, with the sun above”, and the thrift store is totally forgotten .
	The story tells us (with a graceful irony) how our thinking is limited to our experience and how we tend to universalize from our point of view to judge and comprehend all things. Actually, it seems that human beings, necessarily located on time and space, and them confined to a certain portion of reality, nowadays presume and assume a general condition, developing a sense of the whole and integration. This sense of unity and integration seems to underlie many senses of the polysemous noun “world”.
But the story tells also basic characteristics often associated with the definition of the world, or with possible definitions of the world. The tail narrates how the human kind elaborate their concepts, and dramatize the limits of human experience, the confusion between meanings, the hesitation and presumption of human beings; these are boundaries of the mankind, showed by an animal, which forces us to see the limits of our quest for meaning. This passionate quest for knowledge of all things is carried on by a dilettante, which has his mental sanity frequently questioned during the story, and actually becomes sick because of his fragility and his disquiet work . Only Macedo can talk to the canary, and, in the first conversation, the canary, having heard the man inquiring why so beautiful and extraordinary animal was dropped in a so desolate and obscure store, responds that the man was not in his right mind, because he, the bird, was not dropped by anyone, and that the seller was in fact his servant, feeding him every day without earn a payment, and, as it was said, that the canary feels himself the owner of the world, in that occasion conceived as the store and the square cage . Macedo the ornithologist buys the canary, and develops with him a obsessive relationship, in order to obtain knowledge and reveal to the world the incredible experiences and knowledge of what we could name the “world” of canaries . Machado de Assis often mocks with the pretension of knowledge of human beings, and stresses its boundaries, including the very possibility of knowing in relation to our mental capacities, prejudices, imperfections, social conditionings and all the obstacles which prevent us to achieve plentiful awareness of our situation and actions. Some authors think that there is a real commitment of Machado's writings to the skeptic epistemology and moral (MAIA NETO, 1994). In this sense, the notion or concept of the world is a privileged sample of how a definition immediately understood by all can be at the same time one of the most profound and distant.
World is a concept discussed throughout the centuries, by literates and philosophers of diverse proveniences, and also by scientists. Despite the fact that people generally do not pose abstract and deep questions such as the very meaning of all things and do not always seek to establish the deep meaning of their beliefs and feelings in a systematic and thoughtful way, many thinkers posed this question, which can be considered, in the realm of abstract thought, a perennial one, reposed every time under new perspectives and methods. Since the antiquity philosophers wondered about the composition, creation and working of all things; they created a method what will be named cosmology and elaborated a mode of inquiring which involves the basic structures of reality and its transformations. The question has its origin in the ancient greek world, in the works of philosophers such as Anaximander, Thales, Heraclitus, Parmenides, Plato and many others, who proposed some models of studying and comprehending the kósmos, or tò pân (the ancient greek words for “world” and “whole”) (DIELS & KRANZ, 1952).
To make a little digression, in the case of Plato (427-347 B.C.), indeed, he was imputed to have being created an allegedly dichotomy between what we perceive with the senses and what we think independently of our sensory system, as if were two-worlds and not only this one which are perceived by us, in which we live and inhabit, which would be no more than a delusion and a shadowy copy of an intelligible model, supposedly the only true world. This supposed dichotomy between a “world of appearances” and a “world of ideas” was widely discussed in the past century as a profound hallmark of the Western world, and denounced as a evasive and pernicious attitude by thinkers like Martin Heidegger (1962) and Hannah Arendt (1978), among others, in the line of Nietzsche's critique of Platonism, rationality and God, that grounds the morality and the way of living of the Judaic-christian tradition (NIETZSCHE, 1986). However, while some denounced the two worlds theory as a deep error of category and a vital mistake, others have saw this dichotomy between two worlds in less bitter terms, or even favorable, such as Immanuel Kant (2002), some early Christian writers, and yet contemporary personalities such as Julian Assange (2012: p. 3-6), who associated the purely incorporeal platonic realm of ideas with a certain vision of the virtual world, the “world of the internet”, which would be in principle immune to control and surveillance, but has been dominated by big corporations, governments and spy agencies that seek to control this virtual word. Finally, there is a relatively new trend of research in which the authors do not see in the work of Plato any distinction between two worlds, a much more interestingly view that must be carried on forward (FINE, 1978; MARQUES, 2011).
In any case, what seems to prevail is the question about the meaning (or meanings) of the world. Such a question might seem at first glance meaningless and useless, and, if carried out, it could look something like an insane undertaking, as the canary and Macedo's friends suggest in Machado's story. But on the other hand, the message of the story also teaches us that there is a danger in being trapped in our beliefs and thoughts, and judge that this is all there is. The reality is more complex, and always seems to be a "whole" inside a "whole". The boundaries of our experience and language can always be expanded, and consequently, the boundaries of our conception(s) of the world(s).
