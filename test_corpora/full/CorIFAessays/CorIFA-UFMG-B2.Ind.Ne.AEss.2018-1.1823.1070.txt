<How a theory is written?>

It is possible to affirm that, since the modern foundation of science, the corpus of general theoretical perspectives has changed; and this scenario can be perceived in all areas of scientific knowledge. For instance, in Musicology the concept of musical topics can be placed on reading and writing processes of theoretical adjustment since its first conceptualization. Therefore, how can we understand the development of a theory over time? This essay will demonstrate that the process of scientific writing is a dynamic operation that involves a silent topography of group based rules.

Musical topics are defined as musical-rhetorical structures recognized by 18th-century European composers, performers and audiences “<as musical styles and genres taken out of their proper context and used in another one> ” (MIRKA, 2014, p.2). Although conceived as a structural-expressive approach towards musical analysis by Leonard Ratner (1980), since its first conceptualization the Theory of Topics can be located in a dynamic process of reading, interpretation and rewriting in Anglo-Saxon musicology.  

Ratner’s inventive theoretical and methodological approach, the topical analysis, responded to a precise epistemological regime (or paradigm) within musicology at the second half of the twentieth-century: structuralism. Ratner’s stream of continuity, full-scale explanation and masterpieces concepts bespeaks a musicological praxis consonant with musicological precepts and praxis before J. Kerman’s new-musicology  criticism.  Nevertheless, it can be affirmed that his topical approach impacted North-American and British musicology. 

Victor Kofi Agawu, one of Ratner’s students at Stanford University, developed and adapted the theory within the new-musicology scope. Furthermore, as the outcome of this new reading of topics, Agawu underscored a new dimension in a recent theory: music as discourse and music as a narrative. Regardless Agawu’s reevaluation, other prominent scholars developed the theory and its methodological procedures and theoretical concepts . Therefore, in 2014, the Oxford University Press devoted one of its Handbooks to the topic issue. Without the generally objective to present the state of art of a theory, this volume was dedicated to suggest a definition. 

In conclusion, it is possible to perceive, within the development of Topic Theory in Musicology, the dynamic process of scientific writing of a theoretical corpus. The revaluation of a certain theory is dependent on what a group of scholars, more or less close to each other, mutual refers in their works – in our example, the development of the Topic Theory accomplished by Ratner’s former student. In other words, as the French historian Michel de Certeau suggest, the usage of “we” in everyday academia life outlines a silent contract signed among scholar, in a conscious or unconscious way, that is responsible for a theoretical bias or methodological praxis changing over time.
