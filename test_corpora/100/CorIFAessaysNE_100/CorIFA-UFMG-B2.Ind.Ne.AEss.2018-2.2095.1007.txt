Oral language development is central to humans. It allows effective comunication between children and her parents, in a first moment, and, later, with other members of her comunity. Moreover, oral language is important for reading and writing subsequent learning, which enables the individual’s contact with ideas and knowledge produced throughout human history and which is relevant for his future academic and professional success and for his full access to culture. However, although the oral language acquisiton occurs naturally and effortlessly in typically developing children, variations in the size of child’s vocabulary up to age 3 are associated with both characteristics of the child’s environment and their reading and writing future skills (Hart & Risley, 1995; Hoff, 2003; Lee, 2011)⁠.

In a classic study published in 1995, Betty Hart e Todd R. Risley found that a considerable amount of the variation in vocabulary of 3-year-old children was accounted by variations in the quantity of different words which their parents directed to them. These authors followed 42 children, from 7 months to 3 years old, and their families. They visited the families homes monthly and recorded their activities and interactions for one hour. Subsequently, they transcribed and coded all the speech the children listened and talked and, thus, they were able to observe, in a naturalistic way, samples of linguistic input received by children and her vocabulary growth. The data showed that poorest children heard, on average, a half of the words heard by middle-class children and a third of the words addressed to upper-class children. According to the authors, this could mean a difference of 30 million words between poorest and richest children in the end of the fourth year of life. They reassessed the children 5 years later and the differences remained: when the children had 9 or 10 years old, skills like reading comprehension and vocabulary was still lower in who heard fewer words up to 3. Similar results were found by authors such as Erika Hoff (2003). 

Hart & Risley’s study received some methodological and theoretical criticism. According to Yana Kuchirko (2017)⁠, the authors described lower socioeconomic families practices within a deficit perspective. Besides that, their vocabulary measure could be reflecting the children talkativeness and not her actual vocabulary growth. Respect to the former, maybe it’s true, but Hart and Risley reported quality interactions and care in all families and every child was competent, at age 3, to communicate with her community members. Moreover, differences in children vocabulary related with socioeconomic status weren’t found only by Hart and Risley and it was widely acknowledged before this study (e. g. Snow, 1972)⁠. Regarding to the later, it is important to note that evaluate small children’s vocabulary is a hard task and other kinds of measures have its own limitations. Naturalistic observation enables the researchers to hear unusual words to the general population which can be, despite that, common in a specific family or cultural group. Standardized tests can be impossible to apply in younger children and don’t show this words.

More recent studies, with different methodological designs, bring us aditional information for a better understanding of environmental influences on language development. The notion that shared reading of books with infants stimulates his vocabulary acquisition is virtually a consensus. Mendelsohn et al. (2018)⁠, for example, encouraged groups of brasilian parents to read aloud for their children and found that children of this “lectors groups” presented higher scores of receptive vocabulary than who participate in a control group. Burchinal et al. (2000)⁠, furthermore, showed that receiving high-quality stimulation and care in a day-care center minimizes risks for language and cognitive development in children from low stimulating environments. Both studies, as many others, show that it’s possible influence vocabulary acquisition modifying the language stimuli and activities which children are exposed.

In conclusion, evidences that it’s possible to improve oral language development in infants and toddlers enriching her immediate environment are abundant. This improvement, furthermore, is often and stable in time, predicting future academic achievements. Consequently, promoting the quality of children’s initial environment, whether through policies to support parenting practices in home or providing quality child-cares, should be a priority for politicians, educators and society. Encouraging storytelling with children's books, conversations, and singing are examples of simple, inexpensive, and effective prectices to promote it.
