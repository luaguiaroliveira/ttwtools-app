<Title: The consequences of police-induced false confessions>

Would you confess to a crime you didn’t commit? Although it seems strange and very unusual, a large number of high-profile cases, that individuals are punished by a crime they didn’t commit, is reported in media in the latest years. A false confession is an open declaration plus a detailed description of how and why the crime was committed, despite the fact that the person did not what she is confessing. This essay will discuss the causes and correlates of false confessions, explaining why is it probable to occur, how they tend to be and who is more susceptible to be the victim of it. I will first classify and demonstrate the causes, and secondly, clarify the reasons of some vulnerable suspects agree with the wrong judgment of themselves. Finally, I will expose the consequences of police-induced false confessions and ways of improving the interrogation with the aim of avoiding the punishment of innocent people.


It is not possible to indicate one particular reason for the occurrence of false confession, and also there is not one single type of false confession. Although, psychological coercion is a characteristic that is usually involved in the cases of police-induced false confessions (Zimbardo P, 1971 pp 492–508). In particular, three sequential errors that lead to wrongful conviction can be pointed out. 


First, the misclassification error, which is the first mistake that happens when an innocent person is judged as guilty. The most noticeable argument for this mistake is inaccurate investigation training. Police interrogators are not able to distinguish truthful and false denials of guilty at levels greater than chance, according to specific studies (Hartwig M, Granhag PA, et al, 2004, 429 –56), (Kassin S, 1999, 499 –516); indeed, they routinely make untruthful judgments. According to Kassin and Gudjonsson (2004), police detectives and other professional lie-catchers are accurate only 45 to 60 percent of the time. However, there are other reasons for misclassification. The suspect may, for example, be the preferably noted person who seems to the general description given by an eyewitness or even be targeted based on widespread crime-related schemas, including likely motives for the crime as well as the author likely to have such motives (Davis D, Follette W, 2002).


Secondly, there is the coercion error, which happens during the accusatorial interrogation. At this moment, detectives and polices use techniques that compel the suspect to think that he has no choice but to comply with the interrogators’ demand. These techniques usually consist of promises of mercy and threats of heavy treatment. Furthermore, the interrogation is designed to be exhausting and disagreeable, and the person who is interrogated tends to get stressed for being in this place for such a long time. When a suspect perceives that he has no choice but to comply, his resultant compliance and confession are, by definition, involuntary and the product of coercion (Ofshe R, Leo R, 1997, 979 –1122).


The third error that occurs during a police-elicited false confession is the contamination error. The postadmission narrative is a subsequent narrative which makes the story appear to contribute to the suspect’s guilt and it is an important phase of the interrogation. Police detectives often use scenario-based inducements as a way of attributing a motive to the suspect, typically one that minimizes his culpability, one that the suspect agrees to and then repeats, even if it is completely inaccurate (Richard A. Leo, 2009). Meanwhile, the suspect, even though did not know the details of the crime before the interrogation, accept the police version and agrees with all the facts that he truly, did not participate. 


Another important point of discussion is to understand who are the people more susceptible to confess a crime they did not commit. Common personality factors are identified in individuals who have poor memories, high levels of anxiety, low self-esteem, and low assertiveness. For instance, these characteristics are detected in develop-mentally disable or cognitively impaired, juveniles, and mentally ill. These people tend to lack the ability to think about the consequences of their actions and are easily overwhelmed by stress. Also, they often have a high need for approval and desire to please, especially to these authority figures that demand a certain answer (Gudjonsson G, Clare I, Rutter S, et al, 1993). 


Finally, it is substantial to evidence of the consequences of police-induced false confessions. The presence of a confession usually confirms a certain point of view and contaminates all the further interpretation of facts by both officials and jurors – they tend to interpret all the other information in the worst possible light for the defendant. A suspect’s confession sets in motion a seemingly irrefutable presumption of guilt among justice officials, the media, the public, and lay jurors (Leo R, Ofshe R, 1998). Once the confession is acquired, the police commonly close their investigation, clear the case as solved, and make no effort to pursue any exculpatory evidence or other possible leads, even if the confession is internally inconsistent, contradicted by external evidence, or the result of coercive interrogation (Leo R, 1996, 266 –303). In addition, prosecutors rarely consider the chance to admit the innocence of a suspect who has falsely confessed, and some of them refuse the error even after DNA evidence has proved the mistake. Conditioned to disbelieve defendants’ claims of innocence or police misconduction, judges rarely suppress confessions, even highly questionable ones (Givelber D, 2000). 


Because of the methods of psychological interrogation, it is possible to conclude that false confessions are not extraordinary, and unquestionably a great number of innocent people confess falsely to serious crimes. However, is crucial to discover techniques to change this result, as well as expose to people that the thought of <direct citation deleted> (Leo R, 2008) it’s not accurate. One of the solutions for the problem is videotape the whole police interrogations, as this can induce an empirical and comprehensive record, that can be reviewed by everyone. Also, it is vital an improvement of police training about false confessions, including putting time limits on interrogations, and remodel the way of bluffing – instead of lie about possible DNA evidence and judge the person as already guilty, say something near to “You don’t know what I know, I will ask you some questions and look for the right answers, but if you lie, we are done and I cannot help you anymore”. Finally, provide additional safeguards for vulnerable populations such as the developmentally disabled and juveniles (Leo R, 2008). As a result of such reforms, the criminal justice could be improved and we must fight for it as possible victims, as possible suspects and as members of a civilized society that wants the actual perpetrator to be punished, and no one else.
