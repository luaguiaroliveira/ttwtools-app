<Theory Fandom meets activism: Rethinking civic and political participation>

Shresthova and Brough (2012) describes fan activism as a new way of understanding media participation in civic and political contexts. In one hand, according to Jenkis's (2006) media studies, a fandom is a group of people that combines affinity in general pop culture, including music, movies, and books. On the other hand, activism is understood as an action that promotes changes in a hegemonic situation; occurs in environmental or women's rights struggles, for example. As well, we have fan activism: a group of people who use their affinity in pop culture to bring about political or civic change. In this essay, I will bring three reasons why the study of Shresthova and Brough, along with Jenkis's extensive work, is important to the media communication field.
First, it is important to consider how fan activism has been associated in most surveys. Shresthova and Brough explains that fan activism is still more often understood as active fans lobbying for a content related outcome, such as a program staying on the air or the representation of racial or sexual minorities. In deed, that is fan activism, however, that definition neither includes all humanitarian actions of the fans, nor respects their movements as civic participation. To illustrate this, we can cite the so-called ""Avatar Activism"": where overland Palestinian activists marched in dressed as the characters in the Avatar blockbuster. The intention was successful, they drew a lot of attention to their fight. So the impact here was on broadening the conception of fan activism.
Second, the discussion between how audience accepts some content. Shresthova and Brough contributes to problematizing the idea that the public has a passive receptor performance. As passive performance of the receiver, they would not be able to criticize, military by a flag or, most importantly, nor choose what they prefer to consume. In English Cultural Studies's view, just by changing the television channel, the public is making an important decision, saying no and yes. More contemporaneously, in the news media era, users not only accept content, but also complain in groups, propose actions to change something they think is wrong, and create their own content by uploading on youtube or instagram, for instance. In addition to humanitarian actions that we are studying here. All of these cases lead us to comprehend the power of reaction and action that fans have in participatory culture (Jenkis, 2006).
After assuming what fan activism is and how it works autonomously, Shresthova and Brough argues that fan activism is a rich basis for studying the contemporary dynamics of civic participation. That is our third reason. The Harry Potter Alliance (HPA), for example, created the Granger Leadership Academy, the world's first and only refuge for fan activists, and constructed several new libraries in underserved communities around the world. It demonstrates how Shresthova and Brough are right. In a connected world, the booming social media and internet applications affect the way we act collectively, including civic and political participation. So, the pop culture is being used as a political action and it is a significant mode of changing hegemonies and situations in contemporary world. 
Finally, as discussed in this essay, we can conclude some points that contributes for media communication field. Fan activism estresses in participatory culture, audience action and civic and political involvement. In this case, all of these areas are working together to allow a group that has shared tastes to operate through shared struggles as well. Fan activism is a new area in communication that deserves our attention as researchers.
