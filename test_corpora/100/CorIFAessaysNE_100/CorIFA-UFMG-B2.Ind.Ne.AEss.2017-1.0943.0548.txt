<Can The World Get Along Without Natural Resources?>

It is widely agreed that the well-known classic work of Solow (1956) and his aggregate production function are important in growth theory. However, Solow’s production function only takes into account the inputs capital and labor. This neoclassical production function is a mathematically clear way of saying that ‘ <the world can, in effect, get along without natural resources> ’ (Solow, 1974). It is because Solow's proposal suggests that there is quite a lot of substitutability between natural resources and capital. Based on an example that has become classic, someone might think: is it possible to make a cake without ingredients, only with the oven and cook? Furthermore, is it possible to substitute ingredients for ovens? The problem is that capital and natural resources (or ovens and ingredients) are not substitutes but complementary, and any theory that does not consider the importance of natural resources in the world cannot be, in fact, close to reality. It will be refuted here three justifications for not considering natural resources in that production function. First, the justification that the resources are not really scarce. Second, the justification that natural resources can be perfectly substituted for reproducible capital. Finally, the justification that new technologies would provide the same amount of product/service using fewer inputs.
First, Solow’s production function is far from reality because Solow (1974) does not believe that resources are in fact scarce. Consequently, they should stay out of neoclassical production function. Some, in fact, say that resources are not really scarce. For instance, Barnett & Morse (1963) disregarded the resources’ importance when saying that nature imposes particular scarcities, but not an inescapable general scarcity. Who believe on that always uses the example of air as a free good. However, most resources are not free goods and there is often no way to price them.
Another reason for the distance to reality is that it is considered by Solow (1974) that natural resources can be perfectly substituted. On the one hand, Solow (1974) and Stiglitz (1979) took into account, in their production function the productive inputs capital and labor only; with the justification that capital and natural resources are substitutes in some way. On the other hand, using Georgescu-Roegen (1975, 1979), Daly (1997) showed the need to take natural resources into account. It is considered by Georgescu-Roegen (1975, 1979) and Daly (1997) that Solow (1956, 1974) and Stiglitz’s (1979) proposal does not deal with the substitution of exhaustible resources by renewable resources, as Nordhaus & Tobin (1972), but rather the substitution of resources for capital. The point is that it is considered by many, as Georgescu-Roegen (1975, 1979) and Daly (1997), that capital and resources are not substitute, but complementary.
Finally, neoclassical production function is far from reality because Solow (1974) argues that new technologies would provide the same amount of output using less input. Ayres (1996) defends Solow (1974) stating that in the future, economic system will no longer need to produce significant quantity of material goods. If it is true, there is no absolute minimum finite input required to produce one unit of welfare. Daly (1997) shows that it is not about welfare, but even about physical production.
In conclusion, neoclassical production function and other points of view should be further discussed. But in the end, then, one thing is clear: in order to avoid it to be mere paper-and-pencil operation, some should at least include natural resources as inputs and waste as one of the outputs.
