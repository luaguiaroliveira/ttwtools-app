<Fungal biotransformation of natural products and drugs
XXX / IFA III>

Throughout the history, filamentous fungi have been used in many biotechnological applications, such as for direct alimentation (mushrooms and truffles), fermentation process for beverages and bakery products, and drugs and cosmetics production. The production of several bioactive secondary metabolites by these organisms enables these applications, and considering the existing biological variety, there are several possibilities for studying. Other interest application is the biotransformation process, which consist in the use of fungi species for chemical transformations in organic compounds, process carried out by enzymes produced by the fungi (TAKAHASHI et al., 2017). The technique highlights the functionalization of natural products containing non-activated carbons, such as the introduction of hydroxyl groups and other organic functions (TAKAHASHI et al., 2014). This approach aims to obtain new bioactive compounds and also to remove or reduce pollutants contents in aquatic environments in a more sustainable way.

Firstly, the fungal biotransformation process can be considered as an alternative chemical synthesis for the production of bioactive molecules, especially when the desired structural modifications demands many steps and high use of solvents using classical synthetic methods or even cannot be performed (TAKAHASHI et al., 2017). Dos Santos and colleagues (2018) obtained three other compounds from the interaction of the natural diterpene trachyloban-19-oic acid with the fungus Syncephalastrum racemosum. The new compounds were tested as acetylcholinesterase (AChE) inhinitors, the main enzyme related to the Alzheimer’s disease. Interestingly, these compounds presented higher activity than the starting material trachyloban-19-oic acid. In addition, one of them showed even higher AChE inhibition than the standard used in the test, the anticholinesterase agent galantamine. For the same compound, other fungi species showed efficiency in previous works, as for example, Rhizopus stolonifer, whose experiment generated the synthesis of six new chemical compounds of biological interest (SILVA et al., 2002). Another example is the biotransformation of the compound 20-(S)-protopanaxatriol by the fungus Mucor racemosus, providing six new compounds. The anti-cancer activity bioassay for these compounds disclosed potent inhibitory activity of two of them against SH-SY5Y and HepG2 cell lines (CHEN et al., 2015). These examples shows the claims proposed by the technique being efficiently achieved.

Another approach for this methodology is for environmental applications. In these cases, the process can be also called as bioremediation, and the purpose of structural modifications is to degrade organic contaminants, such as pesticides, dyes, pharmaceuticals, and personal care products, or at least to decrease their toxicity or accumulation in the environment (TAKAHASHI et al., 2014). A successful example was the use of the fungus Trametes versicolor, commonly grown in rice husk, for the degradation of the pesticide carbofuran. The process reached 55.1% of degradation, and was detected that the biotransformation product is 89% less toxic when compared to the starting material, carbofuran (RUIZ-HIDALGO et al., 2014). In recent works, Syncephalastrum racemosum strains showed promising results for chemical transformations in the molecule of ibuprofen, a prescription-free nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug, widely used throughout the world and, for that reason, commonly present in the water bodies. This process is in development, aiming for assist the removal of this drug from environmental aqueous matrices (MARTINS and TAKAHASHI, 2019). An important factor is that the bioremediation process does not alter the physicochemical properties of the treatment site and can be used as an alternative for physical and chemical processes that are not applicable in some situations (MARTINS et al., 2019). 

A considerable advantage for the biotransformation process is the fact that it is a green methodology. The use of solvents and reagents are reduced, as well as the production of waste that can be harmful to human health and for the environment is low. Fungal biomass are produced in high yields and consists in biodegradable organic material, and many fungi species are officially considered safe to use. In addition, the use of this sustainable approach is advantageous because of the lower cost of fungal manipulation, in relation to the cost of enzymes isolation, for example. Fungal operation is simple and fast, and with successive cell reintegrations, a single fungal strain can be used during years in several interdisciplinary studies for exploration of the fungal metabolism. As fungi do not depend on seasonality for their growth, they can be replicated throughout the year and the process can be optimized by modifying cultivation conditions in considerably accessible plant-based culture media as a source of nutrients (TAKAHASHI et al., 2017). 

Considering all these interesting applications, it is possible to conclude that the fungal biotransformation can be an efficient, low cost, and sustainable process. Consist in a green alternative from conventional chemical synthesis and conventional waterbodies treatments, a desired aspect nowadays, where so many environmental disasters and recklessness are occurring. In this way, its use has been increasingly incorporated in the pharmaceutical industry, for the production of medicines of great utility and high commercial value. It also demonstrates considerable ecological and environmental potential, making possible the reduction of levels of some persistent compounds that represent a potential risk to the ecosystem. Finally, the rich fungal biodiversity enables an infinity of studies for future advances in this area of natural products chemistry, applied microbiology, and biotechnology.
