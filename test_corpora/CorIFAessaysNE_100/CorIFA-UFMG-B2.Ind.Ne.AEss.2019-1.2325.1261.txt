<Title: Relevance of taxonomical collections and the advantages of its informatization through collaborative networks.>

In 2007, the United Nations Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD/UN) was launched and Brazil became one of the signatories of the agreement that, herein with the fifteen of the seventeen U.N. goals for sustainable development, defined specific targets and commitments to understand and assess biodiversity through a series of methodologies of which the herbariums and other taxonomical collections plays a critical role as the main source for present and historical data about species diversity in the world. Our country possesses a huge and threatened biodiversity, that ultimately, has an uncounted impact over the economy, culture, medicine and other aspects.  Besides a great amount of species, there is a considerable knowledge gap that must be understood and fulfilled with a range of public and collective investments that, nevertheless, are actually been slowed down in the last three years in Brazil (Angelo, 2017), despite the massive rates of species extinction today. 
Taxonomical collections and species databases are an important tool for society and governments to acknowledge, assess and plan the sustainable use of their subjacent biodiversity. Until very recently, practically only scientists could have access to biodiversity information, without such practicality and collectiveness though. Nowadays, access to these datasets have become much more attainable through the implementation of an online collaborating network. In Brazil many projects and online tools were launched in that way: INCT – Herbario Virtual; CRIA; speciesLink; SisBiota; Lacunas; BioGeo; ABELHA; SiBBr; Reflora and Flora do Brasil are a few of them. Together, they provide free and open access to biodiversity data for society, scientists and policy makers, corresponding for more than 40 terabytes of information, 20 million exsiccate images, 125 national and international participating herbariums and 486 dataset providers.
The fact that we live in one of the most biodiverse countries in the planet is already familiar. According to the Flora do Brasil project (2019), Brazil accounts for a quarter of the worlds plants species, of which about 55% only exists in here. Moreover, there is an estimated 5.1 million fungi species, yet, only 70.000 are described by science (Blackwell, 2011). It is estimated that an extensive part of global biodiversity will be forever destroyed even before been ever known by science, and that is an already present issue (Maia, 2017). The so-called sixth global mass extinction event (associated with the demographic human growth and use of planet resources) may also lead to the coextinction of traditional knowledge, and certainly, to all technological and medicinal potential associated with species entities and their genes, that, without mentioning the intrinsic biodiversity, biological and ecosystemic value. 
As already mentioned, biodiversity implies in many cultural and economic important aspects of our society, for instance, biodiversity is a multibillionaire global market, although the costs of its correct conservation could surpass     U.S.$ 76 billion (McCarthy, 2012). Culturally, every traditional population, to ancient until modern times, relies upon local biodiversity to sustain its way of life, a very conservative estimative suggest that each dollar invested in conservation returns seven dollars to the economy (Souza et al. 2017), therefore, conservation and comprehension of biodiversity is essential for a responsible and sustainable human development for future and present generations.
Improving species and natural resources conservation and sustainable economic development, is already a big task by itself, however, to do so, we must properly access our biodiversity in collaborative ways. In order to solve this knowledge-gap problem, investments on basic science, infrastructure and human resources formation must be made, nevertheless, the Brazilian Science budget is now at its lowest on a 12-year history record, in 2019, the Ministry of Science, Technology, Innovations and Communications (MCTIC) is facing a catastrophic 42% halt in budget whilst public universities are also under threat. Undoubtedly, Brazil is losing biodiversity and economic potential that will possibly never be recovered.
