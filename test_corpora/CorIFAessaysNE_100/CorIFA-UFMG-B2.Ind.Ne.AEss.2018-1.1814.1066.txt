<DISTANCE EDUCATION: THE BRAZILIAN DILEMMA>

Distance education (from now on “DE”), structured learning in which the student and instructor are separated by place, and sometimes by time is currently the fastest growing form of domestic and international education (Gunawardena and McIsaac, 2004). This growing, however, refers essentially to part of higher education in first place, and is kind of distant from the extremely important years of primary and secondary education in Brazil (see Table 1). Under the broad conceptual scope of DE several names of related or equivalent methods and practices have been coined: online education, distance learning, flexible learning, e-learning, online/virtual learning, networked learning, distributed learning, web-based and web-enhanced instruction, and so on (these names can be readily grasped by browsing the internet a few minutes). All these methods and practices have technology as a critical element (Bates, 2008), and one important question then arises: Is the educational environment in Brazil adequate for the proper developing and functioning of DE practices? I argue that the dependence on technology for DE to happen effectively and efficiently restricts, in numerous contexts and realities in Brazil, the range of its beneficial results. To support this claim, I refer to three DE strategic points- purpose, methods and curricula- to show why I believe that beneficial results of DE in Brazil are unlikely to be attained in a general basis. So, I first consider DE purpose in terms of its implicit principle, than provide brief impressions about methods and finally make a point regarding curricula.

Building an educational system is profoundly dependent on the determination of very well limited and specified purpose(s), and DE systems are no exception to this rule especially considering the fast pace that this form of education is spreading worldwide. In theory, the main purpose or number one priority of distance education is

…to ensure the right to “education for all”, but while notable progress has been made in improving the quality and scope of education for many children since the World Conference for Education for All (Jomtien, Thailand, 1990), only marginal progress has been made towards extending education to millions of excluded children, “reaching the unreached” (UNESCO, 2002 p. 85).

It has not been ignored by researchers the need to give special attention to the socioeconomic reality of Latin American countries, Brazil included, regarding equality of access to information in face of our enormous and unfair regional differences (MUGNOL, 2009), what puts at stake the noble purpose(s) of DE. In other words, the purpose(s) of DE must consider the astounding number of regional disparities and discrepancies that we see in a major number of Brazilian cities in terms of social and economic aspects, meaning that the equality of access to information just mentioned does not happen in real life here. Purposes have to be established in relation to this historically unbalanced picture, what is to say, a DE system focusing on literate audiences, for example, will clearly have a different purpose from a system designed to attend illiterate people living in precarious socioeconomic and formal educational contexts. The general purpose of “education for all”, therefore, is better seen as a general guiding principle, since it is hardly possible to develop DE systems that will equally apply to audiences as far apart as literate students belonging to private schools of Southeastern Brazil and illiterate children and teenagers living in the so many desolate small towns and villages spread all over the country. So, purpose(s) of DE ought to be particularized to a certain social context to have real meaning to the audience which is going to receive the input prescribed to be delivered.

Another strategic point that is critical for the success of DE initiatives concerns the technology-mediated methods employed and how adequately they match social, cultural and economic factors in our diversified and plural society. The technological development and capacity so crucial to the implementation and success of DE methods, mostly dependent on internet access, does not account for continually providing the majority of the Brazilian unwealthy population and educational institutions with proper updated electronic devices, equipment and technical assistance. Figure 1 shows the penetration of internet by country, and the still modest position of Brazil in the ranking. The use of technology-based methods by students/learners demands formal knowledge about the operation and manipulation of the electronic devices at hand.

A particular problem with the costs of online education[, and straightly related to the method issue,] is that there is increasing evidence that it takes more time to support students studying online than it does to provide face-to-face teaching. Working practices can affect costs significantly. [For example,] courses that take students many hours to study tend to require more materials, and the range of materials on such courses is often such that several people will be required to develop the materials. (UNESCO, 2002, p. 77).

This makes the costs of DE get higher, a very relevant aspect to determine who is going to pay for it and also how to pay for it, especially if we refer to developing countries. Considering this, it appears evident to me that the only possible way to have DE methods correctly directed to a certain audience is to have a precise diagnostic evaluation of the educational profile of the Brazilian population locally, regionally and nationally, a task that requires long-term planning, highly qualified personnel in massive numbers, and true political leaderships’ commitment- elements not available in this country at present.

Curricula are also an outstanding point within DE because require a very accurate appraisal of the target audience in advance, including literacy level, cultural aspects, and socioeconomic context, which altogether generate a key question: what should students learn? Literacy level, for example, will determine the very basic core of concepts, ideas, definitions, and knowledge interrelations that students are capable to process and understand. Supposing that certain Brazilian isolated communities are about to receive input from a DE system, no chance that they can profit from it without getting involved with concepts pertaining to their cultural reality and socioeconomic context. So educators who are going to elaborate curricula, syllabuses and pedagogical practices aligned with DE strategies must be well aware of what choices they have to make in order to approach meaningfully the concepts and related ideas selected. Regarding this, UNESCO (2002) reminds that lack of infrastructure and professional competence in open and distance learning remain important barriers. When it comes to cultural aspects, there is no doubt that curricula and syllabuses will only make sense if connected to the students’ cultural history and background. Brazil is a multicultural country with lots of cultural subunits yet to be studied and comprehended under an educational perspective. This implies that there is not enough technical information and data to support DE curricula appropriate for such a gigantic cultural mosaic. The socioeconomic context, by its turn, is determinant to help in specifying curricula, subjects, themes, and so on. It is generally well-known that Brazilian children and teenagers have been frequently pushed prematurely to the informal workforce both in urban and rural areas, staying far from formal educational systems. So, if DE is to come to the help of these children and teenagers, curricula must show to be linked to their demands, aspirations, and dreams, and answer objectively to the question posed above: what should students learn? DE is not ready to answer to this question because its theoretical and methodological framework still cannot accommodate the numerous conditions, situations, problems, particularities, and paradoxes of the Brazilian society.

So far, only three strategic points and relevant challenges of DE were touched upon in this short essay. The technology-based central requirement of this kind of education/learning is a practical obstacle in Brazil because its relation with the purpose, methods and curricula eventually proposed by DE have not been discussed in detail, considering all the numerous cultural, social and economic contexts present in our society. If technology is to assume a central role in education, a prior long-term project must be developed in our country that shall consider the enormous regional and disproportional differences historically rooted in our formal educational system. DE technology-based initiatives will only be meaningful if their purposes, methods and curricula are adequately established in terms of the target audience supposed to be submitted to such initiatives. Therefore, I shall consider DE, at most, an auxiliary set of “pedagogic” tools that can help traditional or face-to-face education in Brazil only in some specific learning situations.
