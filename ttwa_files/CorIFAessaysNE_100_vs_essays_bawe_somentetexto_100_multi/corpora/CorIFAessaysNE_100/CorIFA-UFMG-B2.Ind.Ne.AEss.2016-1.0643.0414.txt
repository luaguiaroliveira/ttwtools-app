<English Task>
<The political place of the philosopher in Plato: atopia>

<First Draft>
My research treats of an ancient theme in philosophy, since its origins in the Greco-roman world, centuries behind our era, namely, the relationship between philosophy and politics. My issue concerns to the origins of the problem of the political philosophy. Surely, that is a Homeric problem and perhaps perennial in our tradition of thought. In this horizon, my focus is more restrict, but still broad, because my object of study is the work of Plato and his master, Socrates (469-399 B.C.). That is the general context of my research, and my aims are the rigorous study of Plato and, in a derivative way, a reflection on our possibilities of creating new ways of life and new ways of practicing politics. In this sense, this essay has a single objective, that is, to report in its main lines an investigation in the field of classics, with a view on academic divulgation .
Plato (427-347 B.C.), differently of Socrates, who never wrote anything, composed several works on which he offers, for the first time in the history, a precise definition of what philosophy is and what politics could be (or should be) . As is widely know, Plato in his dialogues deal with the problem of justice, commonwealth, happiness (individual and collective), and some of his works became much famous (or infamous), especially the Republic, in which we can see the allegory of the cave and the weird proposition that the politeia, i.é, the constitution or the community, will not gone be happy and health until the kings become philosophers or the philosophers become kings . This strange solution to the political problems (Plato saw as disastrous the political situation of the Athens of his time, which in his view was suffering with injustice, demagogy, corruption and all others diseases of the republic) was widely discussed throughout the centuries, including the 20th century, where the Republic was severely criticized by his presumed anti-democratic and utopian features, by thinkers like Karl Popper (POPPER, 1947), Hannah Arendt (1993, 1997), Michel Foucault (FOUCAULT, 2008; 2009) and others. In fact, in the contemporary philosophy, continental or analytic, Plato was reputed as the founder of metaphysics, today discredited as a type or mode of philosophy innocuous and uselessness, and even dangerous. Nowadays, the problems that Plato raised continues to be studied and never ceases to stimulate what we can call the tradition of political philosophy. More precisely, I had been studied the notion of atopia, alternatively to the notion of utopia. The noun Utopia is, in fact, a original creation by another thinker, the English lord sir Thomas More (MORE, 1977), who composed in 1516 the homonymous book, in which he describes a wonder land of happiness and prosperity, localized somewhere in the new world, and named “utopia” after his first and venturous king, Utopus .
Although the word “utopia” is an invention of Thomas More, who as a English renascentist was one of the greatest experts of the Ancient Greek language, and coined the new word from his knowledge of a idiom almost forgotten in Europe in that time, scholars often ascribes to Plato also the paternity of this idea of a perfect land, and, indeed, More somewhat acknowledge that, making some direct references to the work of Plato in his novel. Plato, as the comic Aristophanes and even the poet Homer are credited with the idea of imagining ideal lands and political regimes, prefiguring the tradition of utopian literature and thinking . This tradition, although its richness and imaginative power, was also discredit by modern and contemporary thinkers, who see the utopia as a product of the above mentioned field of metaphysics, in which the propositions are uselessness for practical purposes and seems to be anchored in a deep error of category, that is to say, founded in another world, and not the human world, with its imperfections, precariousness and evils. Marxists, liberals, existentialists, philosophers of language and others can be placed side by side in their confrontations of an utopist ideal, in favor of a presumably more realistic view of the politics, based on the assumption of the things as they are.
But my research is not about utopia. My studies are concerned with the notion of atopia , which is an authentic greek word, attested in ancient texts, including, above all, the dialogues of Plato . In these dialogues we have around 230 occurrences of the terms atopia (noum), atopon/atopos (adjectives), some of them assigned to Socrates and his behavior, and some of them equally assigned to the very practice of philosophy. Atopos and congeners derives from the root “a” (privative)  tópos (place), and mean literally out of place, no place, and, consequently, it can means strange, unclassifiable, singular, unwounted, odd, weird, original, absurd, contradictory, impossible, etc (MAKOWSKI, 1994: p. 131-152; EIDE, 1996: p. 59-67; LIDDEL, SCOTT, JONES, 1996: p. 272).
The recovery of this family of words brings with it a possibility of a new understanding of the issue, for it covers the ambiguities, paradoxes and oxymorons related to the determination of the political place of the philosopher, that is, their role and position as to the theory and practice of the philosophy.
	My view seeks an alternative to the dominant schemes in the interpretation of platonic political philosophy, insofar it provides a new angle on the discussion, firstly by recovering an interesting notion marginalized by the specialists (surprisingly, in the platonic studies, there are less than a dozen of articles and one or two books on the question) and, secondly, by indicating that the criticism of the scholars is formed by a binary and insufficiently complex approach on the problem.
In my view, the Socrates of the dialogues of Plato did not cover the easy polarization inside/outside politics tout court. It is true that Socrates says bitter things about politics, which is, for him, the realm of ignorance, vicious and egoism. Socrates goes further to say that the true philosophers must say good bye to politics, and that the real philosophers (in greek: alethous philósophous) must combat to not governate, to stay away of the administration of the polis. This withdrawal of politics is solemned declared, and the politics is clearly condemned. Socrates says in the Republic (520a): The philosophers must fight to not enter in the politics. And Socrates says in another dialogue, the Gorgias: I am not a politician (ego ouk eimi ton politikon). But he says that the philosophers must be kings (Rep. 473e), and he says that he is the one of the few or even the only one true statesman alive in his time, and the one who has the virtues required for a good politics (Gorgias 521d). But as the mainstream of the platonic studies see in that no more than rhetorical flourishes and irony, I see in that the key for understand the complexity of the platonic political philosophy. Socrates is and is not politic, he is and is not wise, he is and is not a teacher, he is an idealist and at the same time he is always acting, playing the politics, and playing with the politics. Surely, we have here a clear contradictory statement, a paradox, a conundrum; but this is precisely the question: Socrates does not dream and expect. He acts, and plays a strange and unclassifiable politics. That is not a wishful-thinking, it is a reality, it's a new place, an always other place (allotopia ) which he perpetually creates in his practice of philosophy, totally confounded with his life (bios) . His (strange) political position is a place that is not, and a no place that is: atopia.
