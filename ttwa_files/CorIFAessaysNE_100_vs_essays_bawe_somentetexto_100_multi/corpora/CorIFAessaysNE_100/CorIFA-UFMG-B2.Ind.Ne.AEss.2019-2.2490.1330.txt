<Preflex Beam: using the DYWIDAG system on experimental tests>

	At the beginning of fifty decade, the Belgian engineer Abraham Lipski, assisted by professor Louis Baes, developed a composite beam with an innovative prestressing method that received the commercial name “PREFLEX” beam (ESPION, 2011). A Preflex beam is composed of a precambered I-shaped steel girder and a bottom flange of precast prestressed concrete that encompasses the steel girder bottom flange. The construction of a Preflex beam involves some sequential steps, illustrated in Figure 1 and described as following: (a) firstly, the precambered I-shaped steel girder is placed above end supports; (b) two concentrated vertical loads are applied at the quarters of the girder span, submitting the steel bottom flange to tensile stresses; (c) the steel girder bottom flange is encased by concrete (the loads are sustained until concrete hardens); (d) when the concrete flange reaches sufficient strength, which generally occurs up to seven days after its cast, the concentrated loads are removed; (e) finally, the remaining exposed part of steel girder can be covered by a second cast phase of concrete, although this step is not mandatory in the construction of a Preflex beam (steps “a” to “d”).
<Figure 1>

	Some important details related to the construction procedure need to be quoted. The first one is that the maximum stresses on the beam cross-section due to the concentrated loads at step “b” cannot exceed the material elastic range (it corresponds to, approximately, 80% of the steel yield strength, fy). Secondly, with the loads’ removal at step “d”, the beam tends to assume its original configuration (step “a”), but the presence of the hardened concrete bottom flange does not allow it entirely.  As a result, part of the tensile stresses due to the previous deformation of steel bottom flange is transferred by friction to the concrete flange (prestressing) – this explains the minimum compressive strength requirement for the concrete material at this stage. Lastly, as the construction procedure demands the maintenance of the concentrated loads for a few days, a DYWIDAG system will be employed to achieve it.
	According to Staquet et al. (2004), among the technical advantages that distinguish the Preflex beam of other beam types with the same depth, it can be highlighted its greater stiffness and, consequently, the increase of the structure natural frequency. This is especially desirable in constructions subjected to loads that would be capable to excite the structure and generate resonance problems (when the loads that act on a structure promote a frequency equal or close to the natural frequency of the structure, it has higher deflexions and motion amplitudes, leading to excessive, uncomfortable and noticeable vibrations or even to the structural collapse). Furthermore, a Preflex beam has good fatigue and fire resistance, and can be employed with span-to-depth ratio values up to 45 (MANNINI, 2002) – usually, this ratio value does not exceed 25 for standard composite structures.
	There is a motivation in enabling the use of rectangular tubular section as beam elements, however their use is restricted to small spans due to limitations related to their height. Front of what was previously exposed and observing the technical advantages presented for Preflex beams consisted of I-section steel profiles, comes up the idea of evaluating this prestressing methodology in composite beams consisted of rectangular tubular steel profile filled with concrete.
	On the experimental program that will investigate the behaviour of prestressed composite beams composed by rectangular tubular steel profiles filled with concrete, one of the challenges is to sustain the applied loads until concrete hardens (5 to 7 days), which comprises steps “b” and “c” cited earlier. It is not possible to use the equipment of the Laboratory (LAEES – Laboratorio de Analise Experimental de Estruturas) that is generally employed to apply forces (MTS actuator), since it cannot be kept on during all these seven days. To get around this issue, a DYWIDAG system is utilized, as showed in Figure 2 (prototype lateral view). Two cross beams are placed at the top of the tubular steel beam, where the two concentrated vertical loads will be indirectly applied at the quarters of the span. At each end of these cross beams, there is a DYWIDAG steel bar that passes through the exiting holes of the laboratory concrete slab. These four bars are pretensioned at the lower ending using hydraulic jacks until the tubular steel beam reaches the deformation that would occur if it was directly subjected to the bending loads. The proposed designed system is very interesting as it allows a safe, economical and reliable form to achieve the necessary step. Moreover, this system admits that more than one prototype be produced at the same time, since both material and diameter of the DYWIDAG bars have enough resistance to support more than four times the bending loads.
<Figure 2>

	Another challenge to be faced during the research is the prestress loss. Concrete is a material that has time-dependent deformations, named as creep and shrinkage effects. All studies involving Preflex beams pointed that these two effects minimize, with time, the initial prestress of the concrete and this loss impacts negatively on the service load capacity of the beam. One of the main manners to make the loss as small as possible is keeping the compressive prestress between 40% and 60% of the concrete strength achieved at the day of the load release – this diminishes creep effects. The other one naturally occurs in the case of a Preflex beam constituted by a steel tube profile, as there is no concrete water loss to the environment (the steel tube protects the concrete core) and, consequently, no shrinkage effects. Thus, although the inevitable existence of initial prestress loss, it can be controlled using an adequate type of concrete (preferably, a high strength one).
	The Preflex beam, as seen, is an interesting and challenging type of structural element. The lack of research employing its prestress technique using rectangular tubular steel section filled with concrete, associated with the several advantages that it presents over other types beams, encourage further studies on this subject, becoming part of a research line that has being developing at the Structural Engineering Department of Federal University of Minas Gerais.
