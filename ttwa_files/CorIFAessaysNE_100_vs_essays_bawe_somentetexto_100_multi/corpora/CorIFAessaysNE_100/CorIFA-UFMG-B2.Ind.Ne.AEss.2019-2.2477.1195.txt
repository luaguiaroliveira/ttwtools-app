Title: Biotechnology and medicine: philosophical aspects of contemporary scientific rationality>

Until the mid-twentieth century, medicine was concerned with health and disease, either from the individual or from the population, after the understanding of life was anatomized, with the insertion of new technologies at a molecular level, leading to a reformulation in the medical field, which are concerned with the ability to control, administer and reshape the vital capacities of the human being. 
The present research seeks to investigate how, in this new technoscientific context, we could define the concept of life, since the problem of life seems to be placed now in a new perspective: that of inert matter, submitted to intervention and manipulation, leading to the artificialization of life. In thinking about the problem of life and its new status, it will be important to retake current thinking of Georges Canguilhem who thought about the epistemological status of the concept of life and these impacts on clinical practice. 
Elias Zehourni (2011) in his inaugural class at the College de France, chair of Technological Innovation, explains that the state of the art of biomedical science leads to a reformulation of the old concept of medicine as a practice in which doctors were asked only when painful symptoms appeared or when the patient lost some important function. This medicine is called by him as ""reactive"" and now it is completely outdated about chronic diseases that become apparent only after many years of silent evolution. This field of medicine founded on the advances in molecular biomedicine is called precision medicine supported by four pillars: predictive, preventive, personalized medicine; and participatory. The central focus of what has now been coined as precision medicine is the collection of quantifiable data from individuals: their genetic predispositions, lifestyle, diet and clinical data which should constitute personal profile maps. The data, as warned by Iriart (2019), are not qualitative data that incorporate the individual's history in their lived health-disease context, but structured, digitized, quantified and computerized data. Furthermore, precision medicine focuses precisely on the ability to develop computational technologies capable of simultaneously examining huge databases. The objective is to provide a virtual representation of the patient and develop predictive models based on known interactions of molecular, environmental and lifestyle data, which can then determine what therapy will be chosen within a group.
The molecularization of life is a process that gained momentum from the human genome project, for which there was a high expectation that genes had a high predictive character. However, the result was opposite and revealed that genes alone do not predict disease development. Scientists discovered that this process would be much more complex, involving the interaction of genes (at different molecular levels: transcriptomics, proteomics, epigenetics and metabolomics) with environmental factors and lifestyle. Therefore, precision medicine seeks to cross-reference all these data (genomic, environmental and lifestyle) to identify biomarkers that can predict if someone are susceptible or not to the development of a disease. So, the focus is not on the cause of disease. 
What this essays are interested in highlighting is that when it comes to multifactorial diseases, chronic diseases, the complexity of the genomic relationship, environment and lifestyle is crucial in the development of these diseases. However, at the same time this combination is difficult to grasp by algorithms, since parts of molecular interactions are transformed and do not operate by biological determinism and are dependent on a number of factors, including the fact that they develop in the timeline, and in this sense are dynamic and not static. Finally, the enormous complexity of the pathological process of common noncommunicable diseases is caused by a complex interplay of multiple genes with environmental factors, which really stands as a major challenge for precision medicine. For Duffy (2016) precision medicine still has little to offer in relation to these complex and multifactorial diseases, having already had some success in the field of oncology.
To help us problematize this issue, we will follow the clues given by Canguilhem in which he will reject the idea of a programmed telos for life. Rabinow (1999) evaluates that the telos of life proposed within precision medicine perspective has an ethological character, treating the human being from a determinism given by the genetic code and, in addition, by the statistical probabilities. Canguilhem will question this determinism by saying that life has as its fundamental condition its capacity for invention, otherwise how could we explain error? For error, understood today as a genetic error and therefore of information, is generally, for Canguilhem, a maladaptation to the environment and not an abnormality that was already inscribed to develop in the future. It is in this sense that his whole thesis of medicine Essais concernent le normal et le pathologique of 1943 develops to show how the relationship between the normal and the pathological cannot be previously given without considering the relationship of life with the environment. And so it is in this sense that the normal is always a situated action and not a condition of life itself, for life institutes norms in an attempt to adapt and preserve itself constantly and dynamically. 
Life understood in this way demonstrates that it may not be conceivable in its generality, but always in its individual character. For Canguilhem, life is configured as a technique, insofar as it is defined as autopoietic activity. Concept based on the  reading of the neurologist Kurt Goldstein whodefines living organism as the one that <deleted citation>(Goldstein apud Canguilhem, 2009, p. 29). Marie Gaille (2018) affirms that it is important to understand here that Goldstein's formulation does not refer to the ""environment"" as a general environment common to all, it is the environment that is sculpted from the being of the organism with the world. Gaille (2018) shows us how Canguilhem insists on this individual dimension of the dynamics that make up the organism / world relationship.
Canguilhem understands life as a dynamic process. Therefore, the concept of what would be normal is impossible to affirm objectively, because it depends on the relationship that the patient himself establishes with the disease and on the normative capacity of life itself in its interactions with the environment. The attempt to biologically objectify what, for Canguilhem, is defined by indeterminacy, still seems to be the objective of medicine in contemporary times averse to the uncertainties inherent in the notion of life.
Hence the importance, according to Canguilhem, of clinical experience in medicine, since it would be this practical knowledge situated between sensitive (empirical) knowledge and theoretical knowledge (science and philosophy), converging them. In other words, as stated by Durrive (2014), the epistemological challenge posed by Canguilhem's work lies in understanding the clinic not as an obscure and subjective empirical knowledge, but in giving visibility to a sui generis form of experience concepts. It is a matter of configuring an epistemological space that forces the objective knowledge of science to recognize in experience a way of knowing with which it is necessary to dialogue.
